What is online reputation management, and is it worth paying for?

Ascent Vision · Published October 26, 2026

Online reputation management is the work of watching and improving what people find about a business: asking every customer for a review, replying, reporting reviews that break a site's rules and fixing what complaints point to. Paying for the routine parts can make sense. Paying for removal promises or review shortcuts does not: the FTC says reputation management companies are not immune from its review rule. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

What the service usually includes

Offers vary, but they often combine review requests sent after each job, an alert when a new review appears, drafted replies, reporting reviews that break a platform's rules, and a monthly summary. Some sell software you run yourself; others do it for you.

What no one can sell you

Removal of honest reviews. Google says you can report any review, but only those that violate its policies are eligible for removal, and that "Google doesn't get involved in conflict between businesses and customers".

Shortcuts. Google's Maps content policy does not allow merchants to offer incentives, such as payment, discounts or free services, in exchange for a review, to discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or to selectively solicit positive ones. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, announced on August 14, 2024, bans fake reviews, rewards conditioned on a review being positive or negative, and review suppression through groundless legal threats, physical threats, intimidation or certain false public accusations.

The FTC's questions-and-answers page on the rule says reputation management companies "are not immune from liability under the rule", and that courts can impose civil penalties for knowing violations. It also says asking only customers you think are happy is not specifically banned by the rule, but "could violate the FTC Act". This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Red flags in a pitch

  • A promise to remove negative reviews, or a promised star rating.
  • Reviews sold in packages, or posted from the company's own accounts.
  • A setup that sends only happy customers to Google and the rest to a private form.
  • Legal letters or pressure aimed at people who left honest reviews.

What an owner can do without paying

Most of the work is a habit: ask every customer, reply to every review, report the ones that break the rules and fix what repeated complaints describe. The related answers on getting more reviews the right way, responding to a negative Google review, getting a fake Google review removed and review extortion cover each step.

As general advice, paying someone tends to make sense when reviews arrive faster than you can answer them, or across several locations. Judge it by whether every customer is asked, how fast replies go out, and whether the service stays inside the rules above.
