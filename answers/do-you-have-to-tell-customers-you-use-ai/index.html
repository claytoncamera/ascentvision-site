Do you have to tell customers when you use AI to answer or write to them?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

It can depend on the state and the use. Utah's code, for example, requires a supplier whose generative AI interacts with a person in a consumer transaction to say so when the person clearly asks. This answer covers that one rule and does not say what federal law or other states require. As general advice, tell customers plainly anyway. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Utah's rule for AI that talks with customers

Section 13-77-103 of the Utah Code, in effect since May 7, 2025, says a supplier that uses generative AI to interact with a person in connection with a consumer transaction must disclose that the person is interacting with generative AI and not a human, if the person asks or otherwise prompts the supplier about it. The question must be "a clear and unambiguous request" to find out. For regulated occupations, it requires prominent disclosure at the start when the use counts as a high-risk interaction.

The next section, 13-77-104, says a person is not subject to enforcement under that rule if their generative AI clearly and conspicuously discloses, at the outset and throughout, that it is generative AI, is not human, or is an AI assistant.

What it does not do: outside regulated occupations, section 103 does not require generative AI to announce itself unprompted; it requires a true answer when someone clearly asks. Federal law and other states' rules may differ, or there may be none, and this answer does not survey them. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Why telling customers is usually wise

In a Pew Research Center survey of 5,023 U.S. adults in June 2025, 76% said it is extremely or very important to be able to tell whether pictures, videos and text were made by AI or by people, and 53% were not too or not at all confident they could tell.

Google's guidance on generative AI content says sharing how a piece of content was created "can help give your readers more context", and, for automatically generated content, suggests explaining how automation was used.

A plain way to do it

As general advice:

  • A website chatbot or an AI phone receptionist: say at the start that it is automated and how to reach a person; see the related answers on website chatbots and on answering services and AI receptionists.
  • If anyone asks whether they are talking to a person, answer truthfully, everywhere.
  • Emails drafted with AI help and sent in your own name: check every fact first.
  • AI-made images: label any that could pass for a real photo; see the related answer on AI-generated images.
  • Reviews and testimonials: never AI-made; see the related answer on using AI to write reviews.
  • A short public statement on your website saying where you use AI.
