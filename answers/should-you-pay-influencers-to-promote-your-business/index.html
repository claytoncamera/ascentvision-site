Should a local business pay influencers, and what do they have to disclose?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026

Paying a local creator can make sense when a creator's followers are mostly people in your area who could hire you. The Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides, its interpretation of the laws it enforces, say a payment, a free or discounted service or another benefit can be a connection that must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously when it could materially affect how much weight followers give the post and they would not expect it. They say the business can be liable too. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

When a local creator can suit a service business

  • Their followers are mostly people in the area you serve, not a large following somewhere else.
  • The post can show real work by someone who used it. The Guides say endorsements must reflect the endorser's honest opinions or experience, and that an endorser presented as a user must have really used the product when endorsing it.

What the Guides say must be disclosed

  • What counts: verbal statements and tags in social media posts can be endorsements, and the Guides define "product" to include a service.
  • Which connections: they can include payment or free or discounted products, "regardless of whether the advertiser requires an endorsement in return", other benefits such as early access to a product, and a business, family or personal relationship.
  • When: a connection that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement must be disclosed if the audience would not reasonably expect it, including, the Guides say, when a significant minority of the audience does not understand or expect it.
  • Free items count: in one example, a tool maker sends a woodworking influencer a free lathe; if a significant minority of viewers are likely unaware, the influencer should disclose it, and the maker should advise them to and monitor their posts.

Where the disclosure goes

The Guides define clear and conspicuous as difficult to miss and easy for ordinary people to understand, and say that on social media the disclosure should be unavoidable; if a post makes its point in both pictures and sound, the disclosure should be in both. In their examples, a disclosure made only on the influencer's profile pages is not clear and conspicuous, and neither is one that shows only after a link labeled "more" when the endorsement shows without it. The related answer on referral programs covers customers who mention a referral reward.

What the business is responsible for

The Guides say advertisers can be liable for misleading statements made through endorsements and for failing to disclose unexpected connections, and that an advertiser may be liable for a deceptive endorsement even when the endorser is not. They say advertisers should guide endorsers on avoiding misleading statements and disclosing connections, monitor whether they comply, and act to fix and prevent lapses, and say that doing so is not a safe harbor. As general advice, put it in writing before any post: what you are giving, and that each post says so plainly.

The Guides describe themselves as the FTC's interpretations of the laws it enforces and a basis for voluntary compliance; practices inconsistent with them may lead to corrective action if, after investigation, the Commission has reason to believe they are unlawful. Their examples do not address every possible issue, and this summary leaves out state law and each platform's own rules for paid posts. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Tracking what a post brings

Give each creator their own tagged link, as the related answer on tagged campaign links describes, or their own code; inquiries through it belong under campaign, with the creator named in the notes. Someone who saw the post but reached you another way is recorded by how they arrived, such as social or search. Follow each inquiry to won, lost or still open, and judge the post by jobs won, not views.
