Why do my business calls show up as 'Spam Likely' on customers' phones?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026

Labels such as 'Spam Likely' come from call-labeling services, which the FCC says some phone companies offer. The FCC also says legitimate callers can be blocked by mistake when their calls resemble unwanted ones. An owner can help by calling only with the person's consent, from a valid number that is really theirs, keeping calls per minute and repeat call-backs low, and leaving a voicemail with one consistent call-back number. The owner cannot control what a customer's carrier or phone shows.

Who adds the label

The FCC's page on call blocking says some phone companies offer call labeling, and that labeling services show categories such as "spam" or "scam likely" on the caller ID display. It says a blocked call may go to voicemail, ring once with caller ID information, or give no notice, depending on the provider.

The page also says phone companies and their partners look for suspicious calling patterns, and that "False-positive blocking may occur" when a legitimate caller's pattern resembles those of unwanted robocalls. A label or block does not necessarily mean the business did anything wrong.

What caller ID authentication adds

The FCC's page on caller ID authentication says it requires phone providers to use a caller ID authentication framework, STIR/SHAKEN, on their internet-based voice networks. A call's caller ID can be "signed" as legitimate by the originating carrier and validated by other carriers before the call reaches the customer. The FCC says this gives people more confidence that caller ID information is accurate and supports providers' blocking and labeling decisions.

That requirement sits with providers, so the owner's part is usually to ask: which provider carries our calls, and are calls from our number authenticated?

What an owner can do

The FCC's best practices for keeping legitimate calls from being blocked include:

  • Always use a valid outgoing number, and never display a number in caller ID that is not yours.
  • Limit calls placed per minute, especially outside normal business hours, and limit repeat call-backs within a short time.
  • When leaving messages, give a consistent call-back number and consider adding other contact information.
  • Check periodically with phone companies and third-party analytics companies about consumer complaints for the numbers you use.

General advice, and what an owner cannot control

The FCC's page says to make sure you have the consent of the person you are calling before you call. As general advice beyond the FCC's: call or text a lead only with the person's consent, and with it, tell them which number you will call from; after a missed call, leave one voicemail that names your business and why you called, then try again later rather than ringing repeatedly; and a short text first, saying who you are, can help them recognize the number. The related answers on texting a new lead and on who should own your business phone number cover consent and the number itself.

Outside the owner's hands: which labeling services or blocking apps a customer's carrier or phone uses, whether a call is blocked or sent to voicemail, and complaints other people have already filed about a number. The FCC's page notes that customers can enroll in blocking services, change phone settings or install third-party apps.

Consent rules, briefly

The FCC's page also describes consent rules. It says they require oral or written consent before autodialed or prerecorded non-telemarketing calls or texts to wireless numbers, and written consent before prerecorded telemarketing calls to landlines or autodialed or prerecorded telemarketing calls to wireless numbers. It notes exceptions to these rules, such as emergencies involving danger to life or safety.

This is a plain summary, not legal advice. It covers only what the two FCC pages say about authentication, blocking and consent, not the Do Not Call rules the FCC's page points to, or state rules, which may differ.
