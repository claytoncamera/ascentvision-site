Is door-to-door marketing worth it for a service business, and what rules apply?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

It can be, when the work is tied to a street: storm repairs, a job the neighbors can see, or a seasonal service most homes need. It usually costs people's time more than money, so judge it by jobs won per hour at the door. Rules apply: Florida, for example, requires a home solicitation sale permit from the county clerk of court for many sales made at the door, and gives buyers three business days to cancel. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Where it can fit

  • Storm work or other damage that a whole street shares.
  • A job in progress, where the neighbors can see your crew and your truck.
  • A seasonal service most homes need, such as gutter cleaning or pest control.
  • A small, dense service area, where a crew can reach many homes in an hour.
  • It tends to suit poorly a service people look for only in an emergency, or one that needs a long visit before anyone can quote.

What it really costs

The cost is people's time: hours at doors that could go to paid work, follow-up or answering the phone. As general advice, track the hours knocked, the conversations, the inquiries that follow and the jobs won, then compare jobs won per hour with your other channels. The related answer on inbound or outbound marketing explains why most small service businesses lean on being found and use reaching out to fill gaps.

Florida's home solicitation rules

This is a plain summary, not legal advice. Section 501.022 of the Florida Statutes makes it unlawful to conduct a home solicitation sale without first getting a home solicitation sale permit, and says applicants file a sworn application with the clerk of the circuit court in the county where they plan to sell. Section 501.021 defines a home solicitation sale as one solicited in person and completed away from the seller's fixed place of business, above a small purchase price, and leaves out a sale that results from the buyer's request for specific goods or services. Section 501.022 also lists exclusions, including a visit made on the express invitation of someone who lives there.

Section 501.025 gives the buyer the right to cancel a home solicitation sale until midnight of the third business day after signing. The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule can also apply to sales made at a home, and the related answer on cancellation and refund policies covers it. Other states, counties and towns can have their own permits and their own rules on knocking where a no-soliciting sign is posted, so check with yours before you start.

Make every visit traceable

Leave something that ties an inquiry back to the visit, such as a card or door hanger with its own phone number or short web address. Record each inquiry it brings as a campaign lead, with the street and the date, and follow it to won, lost or still open. The related answer on door hangers, flyers and direct mail covers the printed side, and the one on tracking leads from flyers, signs and vehicles shows how to log it.
