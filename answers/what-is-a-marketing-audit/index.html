What is a marketing audit, and is it worth paying for?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026

A marketing audit is a review of everything a business does to win and handle customers: the website, Google profile, reviews, social pages, ads, email and how inquiries are answered. A worthwhile one hands the owner a plain list of what exists, what works, what is missing and what to do first, with a reason for each. It is usually worth paying for when you could act on it without hiring the person who wrote it.

What a worthwhile audit hands you

  • An inventory: every place the business shows up or spends money (website, Google Business Profile, review sites, social pages, ads, directories, email list) and who holds each login.
  • What is working: where inquiries came from by channel (search, AI answer, social, referral, paid, email, campaign or direct) and which became jobs. No records at all is itself a finding.
  • What is missing or broken, such as a form that goes to an old inbox, unanswered reviews or an ad nobody checks.
  • What to do first: a short list ranked by likely effect on inquiries, each item with a plain reason and who can do it.

Audit, website review or plan?

An audit usually comes before a plan, and a website review can be one part of it; the related answer on what you should get from a website review covers that part.

The free audit from a stranger

As general advice, an audit nobody asked for, from a company you have never heard of, is usually a sales step: a tool's score, a list of alarming problems and an offer to fix them. Google's guide "Do you need an SEO?" says to be wary of firms that email you out of the blue; the related answer on how to spot an SEO scam covers that pitch.

The same page says an SEO audit "should be about giving you realistic estimates of improvement" and of the work involved, and to find someone else if a firm promises your site first place. It says third-party tools do not have access to Google's internal ranking data, and to check a tool's audit against Google's official guidance before making significant changes. It also says to grant an auditor read access only, not write access, to Google's tool for site owners at that stage.

Before you say yes

  • Ask what you will receive, in writing: an inventory, findings with reasons and a ranked list of first steps.
  • Ask whether you could act on it without hiring the auditor. A good audit should make sense to any reader.
  • Give view-only access where an account allows it, and keep every account in the business's name.
  • Ask how it judges what works: inquiries and jobs by source, not scores alone.
  • Treat a promise of rankings, or of a recommendation from an AI assistant, as a reason to stop.
  • If the audit leads to a hire, the related answers on questions to ask before hiring an SEO agency and on what to tell a marketing agency before it starts cover what comes next.
