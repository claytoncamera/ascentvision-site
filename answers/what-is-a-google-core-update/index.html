What is a Google core update, and what should an owner do after one?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

A core update is a broad change Google makes to its search systems several times a year, announced on its list of ranking updates. Google says core updates don't target specific sites or pages, so a drop afterward is usually not a penalty: other pages may simply have moved up. For an owner, a sensible first step is to check inquiries rather than rankings, and to wait for the update to finish before changing anything.

Google's own description

Google's page on core updates says that several times a year it makes significant, broad changes to its search systems, and that it gives notice of them on its list of ranking updates. It says most sites don't need to worry about them and may not even notice one has happened.

Google compares an update to rewriting a list of favorite restaurants: new places appear and others move, and the ones that move down are not necessarily bad. There are simply others on the list.

Why a drop is usually not a penalty

Google says core updates are broad and don't target specific sites or individual web pages. Its spam policies describe something different: practices that break its rules, found by automated systems and by human review that can lead to a manual action, and sites that violate them may rank lower or not appear at all. As we read the two pages, a core update reorders results, while a spam action responds to a broken rule.

Many drops have ordinary causes; the related answer on why website traffic dropped walks through the ordinary causes.

What to do first

  • Check inquiries before rankings. If calls, forms and booked jobs held steady against the same weeks last year, the change may matter little. The related answer on why website traffic dropped covers that check.
  • Wait. Google suggests waiting at least a full week after an update finishes rolling out before judging it, then comparing with a week before it started.
  • Size the drop by inquiries, not by position. If inquiries barely moved, Google's own advice is that a small shift needs no drastic action, and that content already doing its job is best left alone.
  • For a large, lasting drop, Google suggests an honest look at the whole site against its people-first questions, which the related answer on helpful content covers.

What not to do

Google warns against "quick fix" changes and calls deleting content a last resort. As general advice, a full rebuild in a panic is rarely the answer; the related answer on whether to upgrade or rebuild a website covers that choice. A seller who calls right after an update promising to restore your place deserves the caution in the related answer on spotting an SEO scam.

How long recovery can take

Google says some improvements show in a few days, but it could take several months for its systems to confirm a site has improved, sometimes not until the next core update. It also says changes may not have a noticeable effect, and that positions in its results are not fixed.
