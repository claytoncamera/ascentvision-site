Should your business have a Wikipedia page?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Usually not, and an owner should not write one. Wikipedia's guideline for companies says a business qualifies only with significant coverage in several reliable, independent sources, and that attention solely from local media does not count. Its conflict-of-interest guideline strongly discourages editing articles about your own business, and anyone paid to edit must disclose who is paying. For most small service businesses, a Google Business Profile usually does more for customers than an encyclopedia entry.

What Wikipedia asks of a company

Wikipedia's notability guideline for organizations and companies says a business is presumed notable if it has been the subject of significant coverage in multiple reliable secondary sources that are independent of it, and that a single significant source is almost never enough. It says "No company or organization is considered inherently notable" and that "only a small percentage of the world's organizations meet the requirements".

Audience matters too. Attention solely from local media, such as a small town's weekly paper, is not an indication of notability, and at least one regional, statewide, national or international source is required.

What does not count

Examples of coverage the guideline says does not count include:

  • Press releases, and stories substantially based on them.
  • Advertising, marketing materials, and paid or sponsored articles.
  • Routine announcements, such as hiring staff or opening a branch.
  • Brief mentions of non-notable awards, and places on "best of" or "top 100" lists; the related answer on award emails explains how pay-to-claim awards work.
  • Simple listings of phone numbers, addresses or hours.

Writing it yourself, or paying someone to

Wikipedia's conflict-of-interest guideline opens with "Do not edit Wikipedia in your own interests" and says a business owner who edits articles about that business has an actual conflict of interest. Owners are strongly discouraged from editing those articles directly; they may propose changes on the article's talk page, and new articles should go through the Articles for Creation process.

Paying someone does not get around this. Wikipedia's paid-contribution policy, drawn from the Wikimedia Foundation's Terms of Use, says anyone paid to edit must disclose their employer, client and affiliation. The conflict-of-interest guideline adds that paid editors have no authority beyond any volunteer, that neither client nor editor owns the article, and that other editors can revise or delete it at any time.

It also warns that some paid-editing solicitations have been linked to fraud, and says offers to guarantee that a page will be saved from deletion in return for significant sums of money are always fraudulent. The related answer on how to spot an SEO scam covers unsolicited pitches more generally.

A page you would not control

The same guideline says that once an article about your company exists, you have no right to control its content or to delete it outside the normal channels. It adds that anything publicly available you would rather leave out "will probably find its way there eventually". As general advice, weigh that before asking anyone to start one.

What usually helps a small business more

For a local service business, the box customers usually see on Google is its Business Profile, not a knowledge panel; the related answers on knowledge panels and on what a Google Business Profile is cover both.
