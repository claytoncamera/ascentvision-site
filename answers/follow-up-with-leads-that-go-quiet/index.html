How should you follow up with a lead that goes quiet?

Ascent Vision · Published October 15, 2026

Follow up a few times, spaced out, with something short and useful each time, then stop and record the outcome. A simple plan: reply the same day, follow up after two days, again after a week, and once more after two weeks with a clear last message. Each message should make it easy to say yes, no or not now.

A simple plan

  • Same day: answer the question they asked and suggest one next step.
  • After two days: a short check-in, in a different channel if you have one, such as a text, if they gave you their number and agreed to be texted.
  • After a week: something useful, such as an answer to a question customers often ask at this stage.
  • After two weeks: a clear last message saying you will close the inquiry unless they want to continue.

What to say

Keep each message to a few lines. Use their name and what they asked about. Make the next step concrete: a time for a call, a question to answer, or a link to book. Never pressure, never invent a deadline, and never imply something is scarce when it is not.

When to stop, and what to record

Stop after the last message, or as soon as they say no. Record the outcome as won, lost or still open, with the reason if you know it, and the source of the inquiry. Over a few months those notes show where good leads come from and where they stall.

Why speed comes first

Following up matters, but the first reply matters most. Research reported in Harvard Business Review in 2011 found that companies that tried to contact a web lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as those that tried even an hour later.

Sources

  1. Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads", Harvard Business Review, March 2011
  2. SmartCompany, "Are you letting online leads go cold?" (July 1, 2011), reporting the study's figures
