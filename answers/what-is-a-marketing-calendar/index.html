What is a marketing calendar, and what goes on one for a service business?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

A marketing calendar is one page that shows the year ahead: the busy and slow months, holidays that change your hours, the seasonal jobs to promote before demand starts, and the dates to check results and renew things such as your domain and licenses. A content calendar is its smaller sibling, listing what you will post or send and when. The SBA says marketing plans should be maintained on an annual basis at minimum, and a calendar is a simple way to keep that date.

What goes on it

  • Your busy and slow months, from last year's inquiries if you recorded them.
  • Holidays and planned closures, with a reminder a week ahead to set your hours.
  • Seasonal pushes, placed a month or two before demand starts rather than when the phone is already ringing.
  • A monthly results check and one yearly plan review.
  • Renewals that break things when missed: the domain, licenses, insurance, the email plan and any paid listing.
  • Review asks, timed for the end of your busiest stretch while the work is fresh.

Holidays, as Google sees them

Google's page on holidays lists dates, by country and in the US by state, on which a warning may appear on your Business Profile telling people your hours might differ. It says you can set Special hours for those dates in advance. Copying the ones that affect you into the calendar once a year makes that a short yearly task. The related answer on telling customers you are closed covers the rest of a closure.

A content calendar, kept small

As general advice, a service business rarely needs a busy posting schedule. One useful post or email a week, planned a month ahead, is usually enough: a seasonal reminder, a recent job shown with the customer's OK, a tip, a schedule note. Tie each one to the season on the main calendar, so the posts point people to the work you want next.

A year-end review in five questions

  • How many inquiries came in, and from which channels?
  • How many became paying jobs, and how many were lost or are still open?
  • In which week did the busy season actually start?
  • What did each channel cost, set against the revenue it brought? The SBA suggests comparing marketing costs with the revenue they generate.
  • What one change will you make next year?

Where the plan and the strategy live

The calendar is the tool, not the plan. The related answer on what a small business marketing plan should include covers the plan, and the related answer on marketing for seasonal businesses covers how to work the busy and quiet months.
