What are Zapier, Make and n8n, and does a small business need one?

Ascent Vision · Published October 30, 2026

Zapier, Make and n8n are workflow tools that connect apps, so that something happening in one app starts a step in another, such as a new form entry adding a row to a lead list. A small business may not need one if its tools already connect directly. Before using one, check whose account it runs in, what happens when a step fails and who is told.

Where each one starts you

Zapier's page calls a trigger "an event that starts a Zap" and an action "an event a Zap performs after it is triggered". Make's first guide builds a scenario that sends notifications to a sales team. n8n's documentation home links to a first-workflow guide.

What a small business usually uses one for

Typical uses are plain, repeated jobs, such as a new form entry adding a row to the lead list. The related answer on which of your business tools are worth connecting lists the pairs that often earn a connection and how to decide.

What to check before you use one

The related answer on how an AI system connects to the tools you already use covers four checks (the plan, the login, what it can reach and how to switch it off), and they apply here too. Three more are worth adding.

  • What happens when a step fails, and who is told. Each maker describes where failed runs show up: Zapier's Zap History logs runs, Make's incomplete executions tab holds a stopped run for manual follow-up when that setting is on, and n8n can run an error workflow if an execution fails. Whichever you use, as general advice, check that a failure reaches a named person.
  • Which real details appear during set-up. As general advice, a test can use real customer details, so set it up with someone who may see them.
  • Who runs it. For any of the three, ask whether the maker runs it or you do, and who looks after it.

When a built-in connection is enough

Many apps offer a connection in their own settings, which is the simplest route. A separate connecting service, of which these three are examples, matters when two tools do not link directly.
