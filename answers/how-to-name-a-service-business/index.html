How do you choose a name for a service business?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Choose a name customers can say and spell after hearing it once, that does not box you into one service or town if you may grow, and that no one else already uses in your field. Before committing, check your state's business-name records, the federal trademark database and the matching web address. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

What makes a name work

A customer often hears a name on the phone or from a neighbor, then has to find it. A name that is short, easy to spell and easy to say usually gets found more often. If you may add services or move to a nearby town, a name built on one service or one town can feel wrong later.

A string of service words usually makes a weak name. The related answer on keywords in a business name covers Google's rule for profile names. The USPTO, the federal trademark office, lists as a common misconception the idea that a trademark which only describes your goods or services is effective, and says unique trademarks are "more effective and easier to protect".

Checks before you commit

Most checks below belong to a different official source, and the SBA says these registrations are legally independent of each other.

  • State records: the SBA says an entity name is registered with the state, that most states do not allow a name already registered by someone else, and that rules differ by state.
  • Federal trademarks: the SBA says to check prospective names against the official trademark database kept by the USPTO, and warns that infringement lawsuits can prove costly. The USPTO offers a search of that database.
  • Web address: the SBA says a domain name does not have to be the same as your legal name, trademark or DBA. The related answer on choosing a domain name covers it.
  • Social handles: a quick look at the main platforms, so customers do not find someone else.

What registering does and does not do

How trademark rights start, and what registering adds, is covered in the related answer on someone using your business name.

On fictitious names, the SBA says a DBA does not provide legal protection by itself, though most states require registering one if you use one. Florida's Division of Corporations says its fictitious name registration is for public notice only, "does not reserve a fictitious name against future use", and that it will not deny a registration for a duplicate name. So a registered name is not proof that it is free to use. Check the agency's own pages for what applies to your business. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Name generators

A name generator can give you a starting list. Every name on it still needs the same checks, and a quick ask of a few customers whether they can say and spell it.
