Should a service business send customers a thank-you note after the job?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Usually, yes. A short thank-you after the job helps the customer remember your name when a friend or neighbor asks who did the work, and it gives them one easy way to reach you next time. Keep it to two or three lines, send it within a day or two, and send it the way the customer already talks to you. Send a text only with the person's consent.

Why it is worth two minutes

A customer who was happy with the work can still forget the name of the business by the time someone asks for a recommendation. A thank-you puts your name and number in their inbox or on the fridge, which often matters as much as the thanks.

It is one of the habits in the related answer on getting repeat customers; the questions worth asking at the end of a job are in the related answer on what to ask customers after the job.

What to say

Two lines usually do it: thank them by name for the specific job, and say how to reach you if anything comes up. A third line can ask for an honest review. For example:

  • "Thanks again, Maria, for having us repair the back porch railing."
  • "If anything feels loose in the next few weeks, call [phone number] and we will come back."
  • "If you have a minute, an honest review helps other homeowners find us: [review link]."

Asking for a review in the note

Send the same note to every customer, not only the happy ones, offer nothing in return for a review, and do not tell them what to write. The related answer on getting reviews the right way covers the rules.

Thank-you note or marketing email?

The Federal Trade Commission's CAN-SPAM compliance guide says what matters is the "primary purpose" of a message; the related answer on getting repeat customers applies it to reminders. It sorts email content into three types: commercial, transactional or relationship, and other content that is neither. When commercial content is mixed with other content, the guide says the message counts as commercial if a recipient reasonably reading the subject line, or the body, would likely conclude its primary purpose is to advertise or promote a product or service.

As general advice, a note that only thanks the customer and says how to reach you is unlikely to read as an ad. Once it leads with a discount or a pitch for another service, treat it as a marketing email, with the opt-out and postal address the law asks for; the related answer on email marketing lists those requirements. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

If the customer calls again, record the new job with direct as its source channel, since a returning customer counts as direct; if a friend calls on their recommendation, record it as referral. Follow each to won, lost or still open.

Sources
