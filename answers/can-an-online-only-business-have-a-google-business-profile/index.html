Can an online-only business have a Google Business Profile?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026

Usually not. Google's eligibility guidelines say a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours, and they list online-only businesses among the examples that are not eligible. A business that travels to customers can still qualify as a service-area business, with its address hidden. One that deals with customers only online is usually better served by its own website and by being easy to describe wherever customers look it up.

What Google's rule says

Google's page on business eligibility says that to qualify, a business must "make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours". Its guidelines put the same rule another way: a business can create a profile if it has a physical location customers can visit, or travels to customers where they are.

The examples Google gives of businesses that are not eligible include "Brands, organizations, artists, and other online-only businesses", lead generation agents or companies, and rental or for-sale properties. The exceptions it lists are ATMs, video-rental kiosks and express mail drop boxes, seasonal businesses with permanent signage, and delivery-only food services under conditions.

Where common setups fall

SetupWhat Google's pages say
Customers visit you during set hoursEligible: a physical location customers can visit
You travel to customers, even from homeEligible as a service-area business; Google says to hide the address if customers are not served there, as for a plumber working from home
You only sell or serve onlineNot eligible: online-only businesses are among Google's examples
A rented mailing address you do not work fromNot eligible: Google calls this a virtual office
A desk in a co-working spaceOnly with clear signage, customers received there during business hours and your own staff there in those hours

Why a workaround tends to cost more

As a general reading of the rule, a business that serves every customer by phone or video call, with no in-person contact, sits on the online-only side. Giving Google a mailing address, a P.O. box or a desk you do not really work from does not change that: the guidelines rule those out, and Google says it may suspend or disable profiles that do not follow them. The related answers on why a Google Business Profile gets suspended and on how to verify a Google Business Profile cover what follows and what Google asks an owner to prove.

What an online-only business can do instead

  • Own a website that says plainly what you do, who it is for and how to reach you; the related answer on whether you need a website if you use social media explains why it beats a rented profile.
  • Make the business easy to describe. A clear page on your service, who it suits and where you work can help search results and AI answers describe you accurately.
  • Record how each inquiry arrived (search, AI answer, social, referral, paid, email, campaign or direct), so you can see which channel is worth your time.
  • If you later open a place customers can visit, or start traveling to them, the related answer on what a Google Business Profile is covers what one offers.
