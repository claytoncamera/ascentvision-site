How do you tell customers about a price increase without losing them?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Tell customers before the new price starts, regulars first and in your own words. Say the date, what changes, one honest reason and what stays the same, and honor quotes you have already given. When someone pushes back, listen, repeat the date and explain once. Some customers may leave, and that is a cost to plan for, not a reason to hide the change.

When to tell them

Give notice well before the change, so nobody meets the new price for the first time on an invoice. Regulars and anyone with work already booked should hear first, directly, and not from a post they might miss. A written contract or membership may set its own notice rules, so read it before sending anything.

What the notice says

  • The date the new prices start.
  • What changes, in plain words, and what does not.
  • One short, honest reason. Costs going up is enough; a long defense can read as guilt.
  • What happens to work already booked or quoted.
  • A thank-you and one way to reach you with questions.

Where to say it

Message regulars directly by the channel they use with you; text only customers who have agreed to texts from you. Then update the prices wherever you show them. The related answer on putting prices on your website covers whether and how to show them. Honor quotes you have given until they expire, and keep a copy of the old price list in case a customer asks; the related answer on writing a quote covers what a quote should say.

Two sample notices (made-up businesses)

  • "Hello, this is Maple Lawn Care. From the first of March our rates are going up. Visits already booked keep today's rate, and so do quotes already sent. Our costs have risen, and this is how we keep the service the same. Thank you for being a customer."
  • "Hi, it's Harbor Hair Salon. Starting in the spring, our prices will change. Appointments you have already booked stay as they are. If you have questions, reply here and we will talk it through."

When a customer pushes back

Listen first. Repeat the date, say what has not changed, and offer a smaller version of the job if one exists. Do not tie any discount to a review. Calm and plain usually works better than a long argument, and the related answer on responding to a complaint covers an upset customer.
