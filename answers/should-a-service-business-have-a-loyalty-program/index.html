Should a service business have a loyalty program?

Ascent Vision · Published October 31, 2026

Sometimes. A loyalty program fits a service that customers buy again and again: a member rate on repeat visits, a punch-card style count or priority booking. For one-off jobs, good follow-up and a thank-you for referrals usually do the same job with less to run. Never tie a reward to a review, state the terms plainly and keep them, and send reminders by text or email only with the person's consent.

What a loyalty program looks like in a service business

A loyalty program can take four common forms. Most are small and can be run from a customer list and a calendar. The related answer on referral programs covers the referral thank-you in more detail.

  • A member rate on repeat visits, for customers who sign up.
  • A punch-card style count, where a set number of visits earns a thank-you.
  • Priority booking, so members get first pick of open times.
  • A thank-you for a referral, the same for everyone and stated in advance.

When it tends to pay, and when follow-up is enough

A program tends to suit services customers buy on a rhythm, such as cleaning, lawn care, grooming, tuning or regular check-ups. People who already come back every few weeks or months have a reason to stay a member, and a count or a priority slot gives them one more.

For one-off jobs, such as a roof or a one-time installation, a punch card has little to count. Follow-up usually does the same job: a note after the work, a reminder when the job is next due, and a way to ask for a referral. The related answer on getting repeat customers covers those habits and the extra care a plan that renews on its own needs, and the related answer on winning back past customers covers people who drifted away.

The rules to keep

  • Never tie any reward to a review, and keep the program out of every review request. The related answer on getting more reviews the right way covers what the rules forbid.
  • State the terms plainly before anyone joins: what members get, how a visit counts, whether rewards expire and how to leave. As general advice, keep the terms you published, and give notice before changing them for people who are already partway to a reward.
  • Send reminders and member offers by email, or by text only with the person's consent. The related answer on texting a new lead or a past customer covers consent and stopping when someone asks.
  • Offer a thank-you you can give every time. A reward you cannot afford to honor does more harm than no program.

How to tell whether it works

Use your own records. Count a member's next booking the way the related answer on getting repeat customers describes, and after a few months compare how often members come back with how often your other customers came back before the program.

Be careful with the comparison. Members may already have been your best customers, so a high return rate does not by itself show the program caused it. If the visits it brings in are no more than you would have had anyway, a plain thank-you after the job may do as much for less work.
