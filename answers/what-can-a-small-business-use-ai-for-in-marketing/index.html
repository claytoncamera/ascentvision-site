What can a small business safely use AI for in its marketing?

Ascent Vision · Published October 24, 2026

AI is usually safe for drafts, ideas, summaries and tidying your own words, as long as a person checks the result before customers see it. It is risky for facts, prices, claims, review replies and anything holding customer details. Handing it the owner's own conversations with customers is usually not worth it yet. The U.S. Small Business Administration advises starting small and having a person assess every message and outreach campaign that AI generates.

What the SBA and Google say

The SBA's business guide says AI "is relatively new, so start small". Among the uses it lists are writing job postings and blogs, using "your original marketing content" to develop product descriptions or to generate and schedule social media posts, and, under customer service, writing replies to online reviews. Among the risks, it says a person should assess every message and outreach campaign that AI generates, and to review content so it reflects your business's culture and principles.

Google's guidance on generative AI content says AI can be "particularly useful when researching a topic, and to add structure to original content". It also says generative models "don't retrieve facts" but predict a likely sequence of words, and that using AI to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate its spam policy on scaled content abuse.

Fine with a person checking

  • Drafts of posts, emails and page copy, built from facts you supply. The related answer on whether AI can write your website covers the checks.
  • Ideas, outlines and subject lines, rewritten in your own words before use.
  • Summaries of your own notes or of your reviews, for your own planning.
  • Illustrations, as long as no generated image is shown as a real job, crew or customer; the related answer on using AI-generated images on your website covers the rest.

Risky: a person decides

Answering the phone in your name is its own case; the related answer on answering services and AI receptionists draws that line.

  • Facts, prices, hours, licenses and claims. Each one needs checking by someone who knows the business.
  • Replies to reviews. A draft can help, but a person should edit and post every one, as the related answer on using AI to reply to reviews explains.
  • Anything with customer details in it. Leave them out; the related answer on putting customer information into ChatGPT covers what never to paste.
  • Reviews and testimonials. Only real customers can write them; never have AI do it.
  • A website chatbot answering in your name. Decide what it may answer and read its conversations; the related answer on website chatbots covers when one helps.

Not worth handing over yet

  • The owner's own replies to new inquiries, quotes and apologies. A customer often decides on the strength of that first human answer.
  • Pages made in bulk to fill a website. Beyond Google's warning above, the related answer on whether AI can do your SEO covers what tools cannot do.
  • Posts or messages that go out with no one reading them first. Scheduling is fine once a person has approved each one.

Sources
