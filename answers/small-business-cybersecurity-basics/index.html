What are the basic cybersecurity steps for a small business?

Ascent Vision · Published October 28, 2026

CISA, a U.S. government agency, lists four essentials for small businesses: teach employees to avoid phishing, require strong passwords, require multifactor authentication and update business software. It then lists three further steps: logging, backing up business data and encrypting it. No checklist can promise to stop every attack, but these four are where CISA says to start.

Why a small business should start here

CISA's page for small businesses says "No business is too small to be a target", and that small and mid-sized businesses are especially vulnerable because they may not have as many resources to dedicate to cybersecurity. No checklist, this one included, can promise to stop an attack.

The four essentials, from CISA's page

  • Teach employees to avoid phishing. CISA says phishing tricks employees into opening malicious attachments or sharing sensitive information, and that staff should be trained to recognize and report suspicious activity.
  • Require strong passwords. CISA says to make them mandatory for all users.
  • Require multifactor authentication (MFA). CISA says it adds an extra layer of security beyond passwords, and to use phishing-resistant MFA where available.
  • Update business software. CISA says outdated software can contain exploitable flaws, and to promptly install security updates and patches.

The next steps CISA lists

As general advice, logging and encryption are usually settings inside the tools and devices themselves, so ask whoever supports them to confirm in plain words what is on and what is not. CISA lists these as the next steps:

  • Use logging on business systems. CISA says to log activity so your team can monitor signs that threat actors may be trying to access your systems.
  • Back up business data. CISA says that when you back up critical information, recovery is faster and less stressful.
  • Encrypt business data. CISA says that even if criminals gain access to your files, encrypted information stays locked and unreadable.

If something has already gone wrong

CISA's page also asks businesses to plan how they would respond if systems went down, data was stolen or networks were compromised, and to practice that plan. This checklist is about before.
