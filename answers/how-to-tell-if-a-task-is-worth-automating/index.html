How do you tell if a task is worth automating?

Ascent Vision · Published October 28, 2026

A task is usually worth automating when it repeats often, follows the same steps every time, costs little if a mistake slips through, and needs no sign-off before it goes out. Score it on six questions: how often it repeats, how many steps it has, how fixed the steps are, what a mistake costs, who must approve it, and how you would notice it going wrong. If you cannot answer the last one, wait.

Six questions to ask about any task

  • How often does it repeat? A task done many times a week gives an automated version many chances to save time. One done a few times a year often does not repay the effort of setting it up.
  • How many steps does it have? A few clear steps are easier to hand over. A long chain, where each step depends on the one before, gives mistakes more places to start.
  • How fixed are the steps? If they are the same every time, software can usually follow them. If the right move changes with every customer, the task is a poor fit.
  • What does a mistake cost? A reminder sent a day late is a small problem. A wrong quote or a message to the wrong person can be hard to take back.
  • Who must approve it? If a person has to read the result before it goes out anyway, automation can prepare it, but the time saved is smaller.
  • How would you notice it going wrong? Decide this before you start. If a failure would be silent, you may find out from an unhappy customer.

A quick way to score it

QuestionGood signWarning sign
How oftenMany times a weekA few times a year
How many stepsThree or four, always in the same orderA long chain with many branches
How fixedWritten down, rarely changesDepends on the customer or on judgment
What a mistake costsSmall and easy to fixMoney, a promise or a relationship
Who approvesNobody needs to approve it once it is provenAlways you, or someone licensed
How you would noticeYou see a copy or a count each weekNobody would see it fail

How to read the result

As a rule of thumb, a task with mostly good signs is worth trying. Two of the six deserve extra weight: what a mistake costs and how you would notice. A task that looks easy but fails silently can do more harm than one you never automated.

If the task has warning signs on those two, a common middle path is to let software prepare the work and keep a person on the final step. A simple way to notice problems is a weekly count you can compare with what you know happened, or a copy of every automatic message sent to you.
