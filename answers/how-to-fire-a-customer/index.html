How do you stop working with a difficult customer without a fight or a bad review?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Finish or close out the current job, settle what is owed, then send one short, calm message that ends the work without blame and, if you can, points them to someone else. Keep it in writing and keep it brief. If a bad review follows, you may reply in public, but the FTC's position is that a business must not use threats or intimidation to get a review removed or changed, or knowingly make a false accusation about the reviewer. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

When ending it is the right call

Usually when the problem keeps coming back: abuse or threats toward you or your team, payments that are repeatedly late or missing, or repeated demands for work outside what was agreed. If the trouble is one misunderstanding, an honest conversation can often save the relationship first; the related answer on responding to a customer complaint covers that.

How to end it

  • Stop at a clean point. Finish the current job, or say exactly what is complete and what is not.
  • Settle what is owed, both ways, and send a final invoice or statement.
  • Hand back anything that is theirs: keys, files, paperwork for work done.
  • Send one short message in writing, with no list of complaints.
  • If you can, name another business that may suit them; the related answer on leads you cannot serve covers how to say it.
  • Call or text about it only with the person's consent; otherwise use email or a letter.

A sample message

"Hi Sam, thank you for the chance to work on your home. Once we finish the work scheduled for Friday, we will not be able to take on further jobs for you. Your final invoice is attached. If you need help in future, [another business you trust] does good work in the area. We wish you all the best."

Keep yours as short. Leave out blame, opinions about the customer and anything you would not want read aloud or posted.

If a bad review follows

The FTC's questions and answers on its Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule say you can respond publicly to a negative review and "you should watch what you say". The rule prohibits a false accusation about the reviewer when you know it is false or make it with reckless disregard for the truth, and your response should not use physical threats or intimidation to get the review removed or changed. The FTC says intimidation can include abusive communications. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Never offer anything to have a review changed or taken down; the related answer on asking a customer to change a bad review covers why. For the reply itself, see the related answer on responding to a negative Google review.

Sources

  1. Federal Trade Commission, "The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule: Questions and Answers"
