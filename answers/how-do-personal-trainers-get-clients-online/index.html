How do personal trainers and fitness coaches get clients online?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

Personal trainers and fitness coaches usually get clients online by being found on Instagram and converting on a website: a page for each kind of client, a short next step, and public claims that do not promise results. In the FTC's view, objective product claims need adequate substantiation, and its health-products guidance covers products, not coaching as such. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Instagram finds them, the website converts

Instagram's page for businesses says a business account offers real-time insights, a way to share contact information and 1:1 DM conversations. That makes it a good place to be discovered, with posts that show how you coach and who you coach. A website is usually where a visitor decides: it can say who you work with, what a week looks like and how to get in touch. The related answer on what a contact form should ask says to ask only what you need to reply well. The related answer on what a service business homepage should say describes the first screen: what you do, where, for whom, and one next step.

A page for each kind of client

Someone looking for coaching usually has a specific situation in mind, such as returning to training after a baby, building strength after fifty, or preparing for a first race. A page for each kind of client you want lets you speak to that situation in plain words and end with one next step. Link the pages from the homepage, since a homepage usually works best with its main services each linked to their own page.

The line in public claims

The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance covers advertising for dietary supplements and other health-related products, including foods, over-the-counter drugs, homeopathic products, health equipment, diagnostic tests and health-related apps. In the FTC's view, advertisers must have "adequate substantiation" for objective product claims before running an ad, and should not make claims through consumer testimonials or expert endorsements that they could not support if they made the claims directly. A trainer who sells or promotes supplements or similar products is dealing in the kind of product that list names. The page also says the guidance does not have the force or effect of law. Its list is of products, so whether any of it reaches a coaching service is a question for a lawyer.

As general advice, not the FTC's, describe the program, the method and who it suits rather than outcomes. A line such as "lose twenty pounds" states a result, and a before-and-after photo implies one. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Marketplaces and in-person clients

Google's list of Local Services Ads categories includes "Personal trainer", which can matter for in-person coaching; the page says the ads are available only for certain categories, so eligibility for your area is worth checking. Marketplaces and lead sites exist for this trade too. The related answer on buying leads from lead sites says to judge them by jobs won, not by leads sent.
