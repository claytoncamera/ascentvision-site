Can you make your business logo in Canva, and do you own it?

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026

You can design one there, but a logo built from Canva's templates or stock elements is not fully yours to claim. Canva's Content License Agreement lists using its content as part of a trademark, business name or service mark among prohibited uses, with fonts excepted, and its licenses are non-exclusive. If you may ever register the logo, build it from the business name in a font, your own drawing and simple shapes. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

What Canva's license says

Canva's Content License Agreement grants a non-exclusive license to use its free and Pro content. In its list of prohibited uses, it says you cannot use any of the content "as part of a trade-mark, design-mark, trade-name, business name or service mark", and it excepts fonts from that point. The same list rules out presenting content as your own creation, including claiming ownership of or exclusive rights to it.

What that means for a logo

A stock icon, illustration or template element from Canva should not be part of your logo, and because the license is non-exclusive, other businesses can use the same pieces. A logo built from a popular template can also look much like other businesses' logos, which usually works against being recognized. This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

Canva is still a reasonable tool for the parts that are yours: the business name set in a font, shapes you draw yourself, and your colors.

Making a logo you can protect

The USPTO's page "What is a trademark?" says that "Creative and unique trademarks are more effective and easier to protect." A logo you drew yourself, or the business name set in a font, is usually easier to call your own than one assembled from shared elements. The related answer on brand style guides covers how trademark rights start and the TM and SM symbols.

When it matters, and when it does not yet

It matters most before you register the name or logo, or put it on anything costly to redo; the related answer on whether you need a logo before a website covers when a stand-in is enough, and the related answer on who owns a website a designer built covers getting a designer's work assigned to the business in writing.
