What is managed IT support, and what should it include?

Ascent Vision · Published October 28, 2026

Managed IT support means a provider looks after your computers, accounts and software under an ongoing agreement, instead of fixing things only when you call. A managed service provider usually covers updates, backups, user accounts and help requests from staff. The agreement matters most: check the hours covered, the difference between a response time and a fix time, what is excluded, what stays in your name, and how you can leave.

Managed support or pay per fix

Neither is better by its label. The question is whether anyone is responsible for the dull jobs between problems.

Managed supportPay per fix
How it worksAn ongoing agreement; the provider looks after a set list of thingsYou call when something breaks and the provider fixes that one thing
Between problemsSomeone is usually responsible for updates, backups and checksOften nobody is, unless you arrange it
When something breaksHandled under the agreement's termsHandled as a separate job, so timing can depend on the provider's schedule
Often suitsOffices that depend on computers, shared accounts and customer records every dayA few devices, rare problems or a one-off project

What a provider usually covers

Packages differ, so read the list rather than the name.

  • Updates for computers, phones and software.
  • Backups, and an occasional check that a restore works.
  • User accounts: adding staff, removing people who have left, resetting passwords and turning on a second sign-in step.
  • Help requests: a way for staff to ask for help and someone to answer.
  • Warnings: someone watching for alerts from devices and security tools.
  • A list of your devices, software and accounts.

How to read a service-level agreement

A service-level agreement, or SLA, is usually the part of the agreement that says what the provider will do and how quickly. Read it for these points, and have anything unclear explained before you sign. A lawyer can read the agreement with you.

  • Hours. Which days and times are covered, whether evenings, weekends and holidays are included, and how to reach someone outside them.
  • Response time against fix time. A response time is usually how soon someone acknowledges a request. A fix time is how soon the problem is solved. They are different, so check which one is stated and how urgency is decided.
  • What is excluded. Exclusions can include new equipment, projects, software the provider does not support and on-site visits. Ask for the list in writing.
  • Who and what is covered: which people, devices and locations.
  • What happens when the stated times are missed, and how you will find out.

What stays in your name, and how to leave

Ask for these in writing before you sign.

  • The business is the registered owner of the domain, email, cloud storage and software accounts, and the provider uses its own named logins, not yours or a shared password.
  • Backups are kept where you can reach and restore them without the provider.
  • You can have the current list of devices, accounts and software at any time.
  • You know the term, the notice either side must give, and whether the agreement renews by itself.
  • You know what the provider hands over if you leave, and how it works with the next provider.
