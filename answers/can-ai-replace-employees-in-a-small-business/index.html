Can AI replace employees in a small business?

Ascent Vision · Published October 28, 2026

AI can usually take over steps within a job, not the whole job. It can draft, sort, remind and copy details, while judgment, relationships and answering for mistakes usually stay with a person. In a Census Bureau survey of employer businesses, the most common report after adopting AI between 2020 and 2022 was that the number of workers did not change overall. Look at the steps in each job, not at the people.

What the Census Bureau reported about workers

The Census Bureau's 2023 Annual Business Survey, which produced 2022 data, surveys employer businesses, meaning those with at least one paid employee, of any size; the text of the article does not break these results out by firm size. A September 2025 Census article says businesses most often reported their "number of workers did not change overall" between 2020 and 2022 after adopting any of the five technologies the survey tracked: AI, specialized software, robotics, cloud-based tech or specialized equipment.

When a technology did have an effect, the article says all of them except robotics were reportedly more likely to increase than decrease worker numbers; for robotics it found no statistically significant difference between increases and decreases. It adds that recent analyses found AI has had, "at least so far", little impact on employment numbers.

What those findings leave out

These are businesses' reports about 2020 to 2022. As general advice, treat them as background, not a forecast: AI tools may have changed since, and a survey cannot say what will happen in your business.

A separate, more recent Census Bureau series, the Business Trends and Outlook Survey, measures how many businesses use AI, not what happens to their workers; the related answers on what a small business can automate with AI and on where to start with AI give its figures.

Look at steps, not people

As general advice, write a person's job as a list of steps and mark each one: a tool can do it, a tool can draft it and a person checks it, or only a person should do it. Steps that follow a fixed pattern, where a mistake is easy to spot, are the ones most likely to move. Steps that need judgment, a relationship or someone to answer for the result usually stay. The related answers on what a small business can automate with AI and on telling if a task is worth automating sort the steps.

What happens to the time

  • Count what moved. After a few weeks, note which steps the tool took and how long checking and fixing its work took, as the related answer on how much time AI automation really saves describes.
  • Decide where freed time goes before it frees up: more customer contact, faster replies, work that was waiting. No saving is certain, and it can be small.
  • Tell staff early what is changing and which steps stay theirs. A change to someone's role, hours or pay can raise legal and tax questions, so ask an employment attorney or an accountant first.
