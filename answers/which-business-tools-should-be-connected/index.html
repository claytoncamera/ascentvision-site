Which of your business tools are worth connecting, and which can stay separate?

Ascent Vision · Published October 28, 2026

Connecting two tools is worth it when it stops the same detail being typed twice, stops a step being missed, or shows you a number you cannot see today. Common pairs are a form and your customer list, bookings and a calendar, invoices and payments, and your customer list and email. Tools you touch rarely, or where copying takes minutes a month, can usually stay separate.

A short test

Ask whether any of these is true for the pair. If none is, leave the tools separate.

  • The same detail is typed twice. A name, phone number or job address entered into two tools costs time every week, and each copy is a chance for a typo.
  • A step gets missed. If an inquiry sits in an inbox until someone remembers to move it, or a paid invoice is not marked paid, a connection can do that step for you.
  • There is a number you cannot see. If you cannot tell how many inquiries became booked jobs because the facts live in two places, a connection can bring them together.

When to leave tools separate

  • You touch the second tool rarely. A monthly job that takes ten minutes by hand may not repay a connection and its upkeep.
  • The connection needs more access than the job does. Give each connection only the access it needs.
  • The tool offers no connection, or only on a higher plan than you use.
  • You may replace one of the tools soon. Switch first and connect after; the related answer on switching business software covers the order of a switch.
  • Nobody would notice if it stopped.

What a connection costs you

Every connection is one more thing that can break. Providers can change what they allow, as the related answer on what an API is explains, and a connection can stop without any message. Pick a number you can compare, such as inquiries received against entries in your customer list each week, so a silent failure is more likely to show up. Keep the login in your name and write down what is connected, so a broken link is easy to trace.

How connections get made

Roughly three routes, simplest first: a connection the tool offers in its own settings, where you sign in with your account; a separate connecting service that links tools which do not link directly; or a small custom piece built by someone, which is a job to scope on its own and usually worth it only for a link that matters.
