How do you keep an eye on an AI system once it is running?

Ascent Vision · Published October 30, 2026

Keep an eye on a running AI system with six simple habits: a count that should match, such as messages sent against records created; a weekly sample of its work read by a named person; an alert when it stops; a watch for cost or volume that looks out of line; one place to log errors; and an off switch with a named person allowed to use it.

A count that should match

Pick two numbers that ought to be equal and compare them on a schedule, daily at first. For example: forms received against leads in your list, messages the system says it sent against messages in your sent folder, or booking requests against calendar entries. A gap is often the earliest sign that part of the system has quietly stopped, because often nothing visibly breaks.

A weekly sample, read by a named person

Once a week, one named person reads a handful of what the system actually did, start to finish, and writes down anything wrong or odd. Choose some at random, not only the ones the system flagged. A named person matters: a task everyone shares can end up with no one doing it.

The related answer on keeping an AI assistant up to date covers the facts it works from, and the related answer on whether a person should approve what AI sends covers keeping a log of what was sent. This sample is about whether the system is still working as it did at launch.

An alert when it stops, and a watch on cost and volume

Decide what quiet looks like. If a normal business day brings new records by mid-morning, set a message to a named person when none have arrived. Send the vendor's failure notices to a person, not to an inbox no one reads.

Note what a normal week looks like: how many items it handles and what the vendor's usage page shows. A sharp rise can mean a loop or a misuse, and a sharp drop can mean a broken connection. Either is worth a look the same day. The related answer on keeping custom software running covers monitoring that tells someone when a part stops.

One place to log errors

Keep a simple shared list with the date, what happened, which part failed, who looked at it and how it ended. Many tools keep a history of failed runs, so find out where yours is and who can see it. Patterns often show up in a list that do not show up in memory. When the problem is a customer being given wrong information, follow the related answer on what to do when an AI assistant gives wrong information.

The off switch, and who may use it

Write down exactly how to pause the system: the tool's pause setting, disconnecting the account, or turning off call forwarding. Name two people who may use it without asking first, and try it once while everything is fine so you know it works.
