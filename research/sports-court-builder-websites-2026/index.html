What 48 sports-court builder websites leave out

Ascent Vision · Published October 27, 2026 · Sites read October 27, 2026 · Last updated October 27, 2026

On October 27, 2026, Ascent Vision read the public pages of 48 U.S. sports-court builder and resurfacer websites (pickleball, tennis, basketball and multi-sport courts) in all four U.S. Census regions (the home page and up to 6 more pages each). 36 of 48 (75%) did not say, in the first lines of the home page, all three of what the company builds, where it works and who it builds for; 29 of 48 (60%) left out who it builds for and 19 of 48 (40%) left out where. 13 of 48 (27%) had no inquiry form on any page read, and 10 of 48 (21%) had no tap-to-call phone number. 18 of 48 (38%) gave search engines and AI assistants no machine-readable summary of the company, and 4 of 48 (8%) had no summary line written for search results.

What each finding means for a court builder

The first lines leave out who and where

People shopping for a court are a mixed group: a homeowner pricing a backyard pickleball court, an HOA board replacing worn tennis courts, a school or park department writing a bid, a club adding indoor courts. Many of them will want to know early whether a builder does their kind of project in their area. 29 of 48 (60%) home pages did not say who the company builds for, 19 of 48 (40%) did not name the states, region or area it works in (or say that it works nationwide), and 6 of 48 (13%) did not say plainly what it builds. A line such as "Pickleball and tennis courts for homes, HOAs and schools across Texas and Oklahoma" says all three in plain words before the visitor scrolls.

Getting in touch

A court project often needs details before a quote: the court type, the space, the surface, the timeline. 13 of 48 (27%) sites had no inquiry form on any page read, so those details have to be given by phone, email or another route. 10 of 48 (21%) had no tap-to-call phone number, which matters when someone is standing in their yard looking at the space on a phone. 11 of 48 (23%) home pages had no way to get in touch on the page itself.

What a search result shows

The title of a home page is the headline people see in search results and on a browser tab, and the summary line is the text under it. 4 of 48 (8%) home pages had no summary line written for search results, so the search engine picks one from the page. On 11 of 48 (23%) sites the home page's title did not include the company's name. On 6 of 48 (13%) it was missing or opened with the word "Home".

Search engines and AI assistants are left to guess

18 of 48 (38%) sites gave search engines and AI assistants no machine-readable summary of the company, so its name, the area it serves and how to reach it have to be pieced together from the page. When someone asks an assistant which companies build pickleball courts in their state, a builder that has stated these plainly can be easier to describe correctly. On 1 of 48 (2%) sites, the home page's words only appeared after the page ran code, which leaves tools that read pages as sent with almost nothing.

Smaller things

16 of 48 (33%) home pages showed no set title and picture when the link is shared in a message or post, which is one way a court can get passed around an HOA board or a club committee. 10 of 48 (21%) had images with no text description, which screen readers and search engines rely on to know what a photo of a finished court shows. On 1 of 48 (2%) sites a link on the home page led to a broken page. On 10 of 48 (21%) sites, two or more pages shared the same title, so a search for a surface or a service can show several identical-looking results.

What most sites got right

All 48 sites loaded over a secure address, all 48 home pages were set up to fit a phone screen, 45 of 48 had a call, book, quote or contact button near the top, and all 48 left their doors open to the AI assistants and AI models checked.

Where the sites are

By U.S. Census region: South 20, Midwest 10, West 10, Northeast 8.

How the sites were read

The 48 companies build or resurface sports courts (pickleball, tennis, basketball and multi-sport) for homes, communities and facilities, and each has its own website. They were picked by hand from a public online directory of sports-facility builders and from ordinary web searches for court builders by state, spread across all four U.S. Census regions with no more than five from any one state. When the list was built, surface manufacturers and suppliers and franchise branches were skipped, though a few national companies that build or sell court systems through installers were kept. Two more sites on the list could not be read on the day (the connection was reset, or the security certificate had expired) and are not counted. Each site was read once on October 27, 2026: the home page and up to six more pages linked from it (a median of seven pages in all), with the same checks as the Florida service-business websites study.

Limits

  • A hand-picked sample of 48 sites, not a survey: it says what these sites showed, not what every court builder does.
  • One reading on one day. Sites change, and some of these may have changed since.
  • The opening-line checks are a word match, not a judgment of the writing. A home page that names its area in an unusual way can count as missing it.
  • The "who it is for" check looks for a short list of audience words (such as homeowners, families, businesses, residential and commercial), not words like schools, HOAs, clubs or municipalities, so that row may run high for court builders.
  • A form or button drawn by code after the page loads does not show to the script, so the contact rows may run a little high.
  • It reads what each site says about itself. It does not measure where a site appears in search results, how many inquiries it gets, or what AI assistants say about the company.
  • No company is named on this page, and none will be on request.
