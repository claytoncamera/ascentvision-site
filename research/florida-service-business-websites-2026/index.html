What 96 Florida service-business websites leave out

Ascent Vision · Published October 25, 2026 · Sites read October 24, 2026 · Last updated October 26, 2026

On October 24, 2026, Ascent Vision read the public pages of 96 small Florida service-business websites in 10 regions (the home page and up to 6 more pages each). 64 of 96 (67%) did not say, in the first lines of the home page, all three of what the business does, where it works and who it is for; 51 of 96 (53%) left out who it is for and 28 of 96 (29%) left out where. 24 of 96 (25%) offered no way to get in touch on the home page itself, and 32 of 96 (33%) had no tap-to-call phone number on any page read. 29 of 96 (30%) gave search engines and AI assistants no machine-readable summary of the business, and 24 of 96 (25%) had no summary line written for search results.

What each finding means for an owner

The first lines leave out who and where

A visitor decides quickly whether they are in the right place, and the opening lines are what they read first. 51 of 96 (53%) home pages did not say who the business is for, 28 of 96 (29%) did not name a town or area, and 19 of 96 (20%) did not say what the business does in its first lines. Say all three near the top of the home page, in the words a customer would use: the service, the towns you serve, and who you serve.

Getting in touch takes a search

24 of 96 (25%) home pages had no way to get in touch on the page itself: no phone number to tap, form, booking link, chat or message link. On 12 of 96 (13%) sites, none was found on the home page or the pages one click away. 32 of 96 (33%) had no tap-to-call phone number on any page read, so a visitor on a phone has to copy the number by hand. Put a number a visitor can tap and one clear next step on the home page.

The search result does not say who you are

The title of a home page is the headline people see in search results and on a browser tab. On 11 of 96 (11%) sites it did not include the business's name, and on 12 of 96 (13%) it opened with the word "Home". 24 of 96 (25%) home pages had no summary line written for search results, so the search engine picks a line from the page, and on 23 of 96 (24%) sites two or more pages shared one title, so their results look alike. Give each page its own title that names the business and the service, and write the line you want shown under it.

Search engines and assistants are left to guess

29 of 96 (30%) sites gave search engines and AI assistants no machine-readable summary of the business, so its name, place and contact details have to be pieced together from the page. On 2 of 96 (2%) sites, the home page's words only appeared after the page ran code, which leaves tools that read the page as sent with almost nothing. Whoever runs the website can tell you whether yours hands over the basic facts in a form these tools can read.

A few home pages ask not to be found

2 of 96 (2%) home pages asked search engines to leave them out of their results, and on 4 of 96 (4%) sites at least one page read did. A setting like this can be left on by accident after a site is built. If your home page does not come up when you search for your business by name, ask whoever built the site to check that it is allowed to be listed.

Smaller things

26 of 96 (27%) home pages showed no set title and picture when the link is shared in a message or post. 24 of 96 (25%) had images with no text description, which screen readers and search engines rely on to know what an image shows. On 4 of 96 (4%) sites a link on the home page led to a broken page.

What most sites got right

96 of 96 sites loaded over a secure address, 95 of 96 home pages were set up to fit a phone screen, 91 of 96 had a call, book, quote or contact button near the top, and 96 of 96 left their doors open to the AI assistants and AI models checked. The basics are mostly in place. What is missing is what the pages say, and how quickly a visitor can act on it.

Where the sites are

Tallahassee and the Big Bend 26, Miami-Dade and Broward 19, Palm Beach County 9, Jacksonville and St. Augustine 7, Sarasota and Bradenton 7, Southwest Florida 7, Tampa Bay 7, Florida Panhandle 6, North Central Florida 4, Orlando metro 4. Tallahassee and the Big Bend (26) and Miami-Dade and Broward (19) together make up 45 of the 96.

How the sites were read

The 96 sites were picked by hand from ordinary web searches for a service and a Florida town: small, owner-led service businesses with their own website, such as home services, studios and lessons, coaching and consulting, and small professional firms. Chains, franchises and large firms were left out. Each site was read once, on October 24, 2026, by a script that loads the public home page and up to six more pages of the same site, one page at a time and at least a second apart (7 pages for a typical site, the home page included). Only public pages were read: nothing was submitted, bought or signed into, and no form was filled in. The checks on the opening of the home page match words in its first lines of text, about 800 characters.

Limits

  • A hand-picked sample of 96 sites in 10 Florida regions, not a survey: it says what these sites showed, not what every Florida business does.
  • One reading on one day. Sites change, and some of these may have changed since.
  • The opening-line checks are a word match, not a judgment of the writing. A home page that names its towns in an unusual way can count as missing them.
  • A form or button drawn by code after the page loads does not show to the script, so the contact rows may run a little high.
  • It reads what each site says about itself. It does not measure where a site appears in search results, how many visitors it gets, or what AI assistants say about the business.
  • No business is named on this page, and none will be on request.
